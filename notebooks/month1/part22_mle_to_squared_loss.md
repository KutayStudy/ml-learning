# MLE to Squared Loss

## 1. Linear Regression Assumption

For linear regression, the model predicts:

$$
\hat{y}^{(i)} = wx^{(i)} + b
$$

We assume that the real target is generated by the model plus some random noise:

$$
y^{(i)} = wx^{(i)} + b + \epsilon^{(i)}
$$

where:

$$
\epsilon^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)
$$

This means we assume the residual errors are normally distributed around zero with constant variance.

---

## 2. Conditional Distribution

Since:

$$
y^{(i)} = wx^{(i)} + b + \epsilon^{(i)}
$$

and

$$
\epsilon^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2),
$$

then:

$$
y^{(i)} \mid x^{(i)},w,b
\sim
\mathcal{N}(wx^{(i)}+b,\sigma^2)
$$

So for a given input $x^{(i)}$ and parameters $w,b$, the target is assumed to follow a Gaussian distribution whose mean is the model prediction.

For one training example:

$$
p(y^{(i)} \mid x^{(i)};w,b)
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}
\exp\left(
-\frac{
\left(y^{(i)}-(wx^{(i)}+b)\right)^2
}{
2\sigma^2
}
\right)
$$

The important part is:

$$
\left(y^{(i)}-\hat y^{(i)}\right)^2
$$

which is the squared residual.

---

## 3. Likelihood

MLE asks:

> Which values of $w$ and $b$ make the training data we actually observed most likely?

Assuming the training examples are conditionally independent:

$$
L(w,b)
=
\prod_{i=1}^{m}
p(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)};w,b)
$$

Therefore:

$$
L(w,b)
=
\prod_{i=1}^{m}
\left[
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}
\exp\left(
-\frac{
\left(y^{(i)}-(wx^{(i)}+b)\right)^2
}{
2\sigma^2
}
\right)
\right]
$$

Our objective in Maximum Likelihood Estimation is:

$$
(w^*,b^*)
=
\arg\max_{w,b} L(w,b)
$$

---

## 4. Log-Likelihood

Products are inconvenient to optimize, so we take the logarithm.

Because $\log$ is monotonically increasing, maximizing the likelihood and maximizing the log-likelihood give the same optimal parameters:

$$
\arg\max_{w,b} L(w,b)
=
\arg\max_{w,b} \log L(w,b)
$$

The product becomes a sum:

$$
\log L(w,b)
=
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\log p(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)};w,b)
$$

After applying the logarithm:

$$
\log L(w,b)
=
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\left[
-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2)
-
\frac{
\left(y^{(i)}-(wx^{(i)}+b)\right)^2
}{
2\sigma^2
}
\right]
$$

The term

$$
-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2)
$$

does not depend on $w$ or $b$.

Also,

$$
\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}
$$

is a positive constant with respect to $w$ and $b$.

Therefore, these constants do not change the location of the optimum.

What matters is:

$$
-\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\left(y^{(i)}-\hat y^{(i)}\right)^2
$$

---

## 5. Why Squared Error Appears

Maximizing

$$
-\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\left(y^{(i)}-\hat y^{(i)}\right)^2
$$

is equivalent to minimizing

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\left(y^{(i)}-\hat y^{(i)}\right)^2
$$

Therefore:

**Maximum Likelihood Estimation under the Gaussian-noise assumption is equivalent to minimizing the sum of squared errors.**

Andrew Ng defines the linear-regression cost as:

$$
J(w,b)
=
\frac{1}{2m}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\left(
f_{w,b}(x^{(i)})-y^{(i)}
\right)^2
$$

The factor

$$
\frac{1}{2m}
$$

is a positive constant, so it does not change which $w,b$ minimize the objective.

Therefore, it has the same optimum as the squared-error objective obtained from MLE.

---

## 6. Main Insight

Squared error is not just an arbitrary function chosen because it is convenient.

If we assume:

- the regression residuals are independent,
- their expected value is zero,
- they follow a Gaussian distribution,
- and they have constant variance,

then maximizing the likelihood of the observed training data naturally becomes minimizing squared error.

The full chain is:

```text
Gaussian residual assumption
        ↓
Likelihood
        ↓
Maximum Likelihood Estimation
        ↓
Log-likelihood
        ↓
Negative squared residual terms
        ↓
Minimize squared error
```

Gradient descent is then an optimization algorithm that can be used to find the parameters $w,b$ that minimize this objective.

> **Gradient descent is not the reason squared error is used.**
>
> The squared-error objective follows from the Gaussian-noise + MLE assumptions; gradient descent is one method for optimizing that objective.

---

## 7. Connection to My Flagship

**Problem type:** Regression

**Target:** The continuous target variable of the flagship dataset.

**First modeling objective:** Mean Squared Error / squared-error objective.

**Why this loss:**  
The problem is regression with a continuous target. Squared error is a natural starting objective if the residuals can reasonably be modeled as independent Gaussian noise centered around zero. Under that assumption, minimizing squared error is equivalent to Maximum Likelihood Estimation.

Squared error also penalizes larger prediction errors more strongly because the residual is squared.

**Initial assumption:**  
For the first regression model, I will use the Gaussian-noise assumption as a reasonable baseline modeling assumption. Later, I should inspect the residual distribution instead of assuming this is perfectly true.

If the residuals contain strong outliers, heavy tails, heteroscedasticity, or another clearly non-Gaussian structure, MSE may no longer be the best objective and alternatives such as MAE or Huber loss can be considered.

---

## 8. Final Recall

When I use squared error in linear regression, I am implicitly starting from the probabilistic model

$$
y = f(x) + \epsilon
$$

with

$$
\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2).
$$

Because the Gaussian probability density contains the squared residual in its exponent, maximizing the likelihood of the observed data becomes equivalent to minimizing the sum of squared residuals.

### One-sentence summary

> Assuming independent Gaussian residual noise with zero mean and constant variance makes MLE for linear regression equivalent to minimizing squared error.
